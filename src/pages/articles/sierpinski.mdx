# The Sierpinski Triangle

Consider the following experiment: Place three points $A, B$ and $C$ uniformly on the border of a circle. Then we put one point $X_1$ in the middle of the circle. From there we continously sample points $X_2,X_3, \dots$ by choosing one point $Y \in \{ A,B,C\}$ randomly, and setting 

$$
X_{n+1} = (X_n+Y)/2
$$

ie. a new point is exactly in the middle of the previous point and a random corner $A,B,C$ (you will see in a second why we speak of corners). Lets do that up to $2^{15}$. Then we get the following picture:

We see that a Sierpinski triangle emerges where the corners align exactly with our points $A,B$ and $C$. 

**Suggestion:** Try to lower the number of points to see that the points are really just chosen randomly. For a smaller exponent, the border will not be as clearly visible.

Lets think a moment why that happens: If we look closely, we can see our starting point $X_1$ in the middle, exactly in the center of the big white triangle. We will call it a layer-$1$-triangle. The next point $X_2$ will lie exactly in the middle of $X_1$ and a corner. If we look closely again, we can find it in one of the three triangles, that are just a bit smaller. We will call them layer-$2$-triangles. At this point it is a sensible assumption that in general $X_n$ will lie in the center of a layer-$n$-triangle.

Given this, it makes sense, that the points appear to lie on the lines in the Sierpinski triangle. The larger $n$ becomes, the closer does the center of a layer-$n$-triangle approach a line. Since we draw a large number of points, most of them will be drawn so closely to a line in the Sierpinski triangle, that it appears as if we placed all of them randomly there.

We can even proof our assumption: Clearly, the centers of the layer-$n$-triangles are exactly the points that can be reached by choosing directions $d_1,d_2,\dots,d_{n-1} \in \{A,B,C\}$, and then walking the steps $\frac{d_1}{2},\frac{d_2}{4},\frac{d_3}{8},\dots,\frac{d_{n-1}}{2^n}$, so that we end up at the point 

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{d_i}{2^{i+1}}
$$

On the other hand, to sample our points, we also choose corners $c_1,c_2,\dots,c_{n-1} \in \{A,B,C\}$ and compute

$$
X_n = ((((\dots)/2+ c_{n-3})/2+c_{n-2})/2+c_{n-1})/2 
$$

**Suggestion:** To finish the proof, think about how a sequence $d_1, d_2, \dots$ can be chosen for a given sequence $c_1, c_2, \dots$, such that the resulting points are equal.

## Trying something else

Whats happens if we change parameters in our experiment? For example the number of corners? If we set it to two, we just end up with a line, so nothing interesting there. But we can make it more interesting by taking a point closer to the corner. For example, we could instead take the point

$$
  X_n = w X_{n-1} + (1-w) Y
$$

for some parameter $w \in [0,1]$ that we will call weight. Notice that for $w=\frac 1 3$ we end up with something that looks like the Cantor set:
